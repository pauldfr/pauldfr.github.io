abstract: "Why do things feel good or bad? A standard answer treats hedonic valence as a reinforcement-learning signal that teaches an organism what to repeat or avoid. Yet value signals can drive learning and motivation without conscious feeling, raising a functional puzzle: what is the distinctive contribution of consciously felt valence? I propose the Affective Compression Hypothesis: conscious valence is a lossy, information-efficient compressor that converts high-dimensional interoceptive and evaluative states into a low-dimensional signal for use by deliberative control. The argument draws on incentive-learning and \"hedonic interface\" work, somatic-marker traditions, and functional accounts of consciousness that link conscious experience to model-based cognition. On the view I defend, valence is not primarily a teaching signal. It is a decision-relevant interface variable that preserves what matters for planning (priority, urgency, trade-offs) while discarding source-specific detail. This explains both the action-guiding power of feelings and their characteristic opacity, including familiar misattribution effects. The proposal yields three discriminating predictions. First, conscious valence should disproportionately influence counterfactual reasoning and re-planning, compared with subliminal incentives. Second, disrupting conscious affect (e.g., emotional numbing or valence-blindness) should selectively impair model-based control while sparing model-free conditioning. Third, under time pressure or cognitive load, agents should rely more on valence as a compressed proxy and less on explicit causal appraisal. By offering an information-theoretic rationale for why affect takes a valenced phenomenal format, the account advances current debates on the functions and evolution of conscious experience and connects them to testable hypotheses across cognitive neuroscience, psychiatry, and comparative consciousness science."
